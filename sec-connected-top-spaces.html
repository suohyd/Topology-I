    Section 2.5 Connected topological spaces

    Theorem 2.5.1.

    Suppose that \(X\) is a nonempty topological space. Following statements are equivalent.
    1. There exists nonempty open subsets \(U,V\) of \(X\) such that \(U\cap V=\emptyset\) and \(X=U\cup V\text{.}\)
    2. There exists nonempty closed subsets \(A,B\) of \(X\) such that \(A\cap B=\emptyset\) and \(X=A\cup B\text{.}\)
    3. There are nonempty subsets \(C,D\) of \(X\) such that \(X=C\cup D\text{.}\) Furthermore, limit points of \(C\) are not contained in \(D\) and limit points of \(D\) are not contained in \(C\text{.}\)

    Proof.

    (1)\(\implies\) (2). The complement of \(U\) (resp., the complement of \(V\)) is the open subset \(V\) (resp., \(U\)). Therefore, \(U\) and \(V\) are both open and closed subsets. Hence, we can take \(A=U\) and \(B=V\text{.}\)
    (2)\(\implies\) (1). The complement of \(A\) (resp., the complement of \(B\)) is the closed subset \(B\) (resp., \(A\)). Therefore, \(A\) and \(B\) are both open and closed subsets. Hence, we can take \(U=A\) and \(V=B\text{.}\)
    (3)\(\implies\)(2). Note that \(\overline{C}=C\cup C'\) and \(\overline{D}=D\cup D'\text{,}\) where \(C'\) and \(D'\) are collections of limit points of \(C\) and \(D\text{,}\) respectively. By the hypothesis, \(C'\not\subset D\) and \(D'\not\subset C\text{;}\) therefore, \(C'\subseteq C\) and \(D'\subseteq D\text{,}\) i.e., \(C,D\) are closed subsets and \(C\cap D=\emptyset\text{.}\)
    (2)\(\implies\) (3). We can take \(C=A\) and \(D=B\text{.}\)

    Definition 2.5.2.

    A topological space \(X\) is said to be separated if any one (hence all) the conditions of Theorem 2.5.1 holds.

    Definition 2.5.3.

    A topological space \(X\) is said to be connected if it is not separated.

    Theorem 2.5.4.

    For a topological space \(X\) following statements are equivalent.
    1. \(X\) is connected
    2. If \(Y\subseteq X\) is both open and closed then, either \(Y=\emptyset\) or \(Y=X\text{.}\)
    3. If \(X=A\cup B\) where, \(\overline{A}\cap B=\emptyset\) and \(\overline{B}\cap A=\emptyset\) then, either \(A=\emptyset\) or \(B=\emptyset\text{.}\)
    4. Every continuous function \(f\colon X\to\{0,1\}\) is constant, where \(\{0,1\}\) is given the discrete topolgy.

    Proof.

    First three conditions are equivalent follows from the proof of Theorem 2.5.1.
    Suppose that \(X=U\cup V\text{,}\) where \(U,V\) are nonempty disjoint open subsets of \(X\text{.}\) We define \(f\colon X\to\{0,1\}\) such that \(f(u)=0\) for every \(u\in U\) and \(f(v)=1\) for every \(v\in V\text{.}\) This is a continuous function. This shows that (4)\(\implies\) (1).
    Suppose that \(X\) is connected. If there is a continuous function \(f\colon X\to\{0,1\}\) which is not constant, then \(U=f^{-1}\{0\}\) and \(V=f^{-1}\{1\}\) are nonempty disjoint open subsets of \(X\) and \(X=U\cup V\text{.}\)

    Example 2.5.5. (Connected subset of real line if and only if interval).

    Suppose that \(I\) is an interval in \(\R\text{.}\) Suppose that \(f\colon I\to\{0,1\}\) be a continuous function. Further assume that \(a,b\in I\text{,}\) \(a<b\text{,}\) and that \(f(a)=0\text{.}\) Consider \(u=\sup\{c:a\leq c\leq b\text{ and }f(c)=0\}\text{.}\) Note that \(f(u)=0\text{.}\) Indeed, if \(f(u)\neq 0\text{,}\) i.e., \(f(u)=1\) then, \(u\) is a limit point of \(A=\{c:a\leq c\leq b\text{ and }f(c)=0\}\text{,}\) i.e., \(u\in\overline{A}\text{.}\) By the continuity of \(f\text{,}\) we must have \(f\left(\overline{A}\right)\subseteq\overline{f(A)}\text{.}\) However, \(\overline{f(A)}=\{0\}\) while \(f\left(\overline{A}\right)=\{0,1\}\) as \(1=f(u)\in f\left(\overline{A}\right)\text{.}\) We claim that \(u=y\text{.}\) Indeed, if \(u<y\) then, \(u\in\overline{\left([x,y]\setminus A\right)}\text{.}\) Arguing on the similar lines as in the above paragraph we get a contradiction.
    Suppose that \(Y\) is a connected subset of \(\R\text{.}\) If \(a,b\in Y\) are such that \(a<b\) and if \(y\in\R\) is such that \(a<y<b\) but \(y\not\in Y\text{.}\) Then, \(X=\left(X\cap (-\infty,x)\right)\cup\left(X\cap (x,\infty)\right)\text{.}\)

    Example 2.5.6. (Product of two connected spaces is connected).

    Suppose that \(X,Y\) are connected topological spaces. We show that \(X\times Y\) is connected.
    Suppose that \((a,b)\in X\times Y\) and \((x,y)\in X\times Y\) are distinct points. Consider a continuous function \(f\colon X\times Y\to \{0,1\}\text{.}\) The subspace \(\{a\}\times Y\simeq Y\text{,}\) and hence \(\{a\}\times Y\) is connected. Therefore, \(f(a,b)=f(a,y')\) for every \(y'\in Y\text{.}\) In particular, \(f(a,b)=f(a,y)\text{.}\) Similarly, \(X\times \{y\}\) is connected and hence, \(f(x',y)=f(x,y)\) for every \(x'\in X\text{.}\) In particular, \(f(a,y)=f(x,y)\text{.}\) Therefore, we obtain that \(f(a,b)=f(a,y)=f(x,y)\text{,}\) i.e., \(f\) is a constant function.

    Example 2.5.7. (Interior of a connected space need not be connected).

    Choose two closed unit discs in \(\R^2\) touching at a single point, say \(D_1\) centered at \((0,0)\text{,}\) and \(D_2\) centered at \((2,0)\text{.}\) The interior of \(D_1\cup D_2\) is the union of two disjoint open unit discs, viz., one centered at \((0,0)\) and the other centered at \((2,0)\text{.}\)
