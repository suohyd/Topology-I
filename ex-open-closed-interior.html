    Exercises 3 Exercises related to open, closed sets, interior and closure of sets

    We list a large number of small exercises for practice. Some of the exercises are taken from ‘Elementary topology problem textbook’ by Viro et.al.

    Exercise Group.

    State whether the following statements are true or false. Justify your answers.

    1.

      Let \(a,b\in\R\text{.}\) The interval \((a,b)\) is an open subset in \(\R_\ell\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • One may approximate \(a\) from the right, for instance, by considering \(a+1/n\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • One may approximate \(a\) from the right, for instance, by considering \(a+1/n\text{.}\)

    2.

      Let \(a,b\in\R\text{.}\) The interval \([a,b)\) is a closed subset of \(\R\) in the usual topology.

    • True.

    • If \([a,b)\) were closed then, its complement \((-\infty,a)\cup[b,\infty)\) will be open. However, any open interval of \(\R\) containing \(b\) will contain points other than \(\R\setminus[a,b)\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • If \([a,b)\) were closed then, its complement \((-\infty,a)\cup[b,\infty)\) will be open. However, any open interval of \(\R\) containing \(b\) will contain points other than \(\R\setminus[a,b)\text{.}\)

    3.

      If the interval \([0,2]\) is given the subspace topology from \(\R\) then, \([0,1)\) is an open subset of \([0,2]\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • We have \([0,1)=(-1,1)\cap [0,2]\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • We have \([0,1)=(-1,1)\cap [0,2]\text{.}\)

    4.

      If \(A\) is a closed subset of \(\R^2\) then, \(x+A=\{x+a:a\in A\}\) is a closed subset of \(\R^2\) for every \(x\in\R^2\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • Let \(y\in\R^2\) be a limit point of \(x+A\text{.}\) Thus, there exists a sequence \(x+a_n\to y\text{,}\) i.e., \(a_n\to y-x\text{,}\) i.e., \(y-x\) is a limit point of \(A\text{.}\) As \(A\) is closed, \(y-x\in A\text{.}\) Hence, there exists \(a\in A\) such that \(a=y-x\text{,}\) i.e., \(y=x+a\in x+A\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Let \(y\in\R^2\) be a limit point of \(x+A\text{.}\) Thus, there exists a sequence \(x+a_n\to y\text{,}\) i.e., \(a_n\to y-x\text{,}\) i.e., \(y-x\) is a limit point of \(A\text{.}\) As \(A\) is closed, \(y-x\in A\text{.}\) Hence, there exists \(a\in A\) such that \(a=y-x\text{,}\) i.e., \(y=x+a\in x+A\text{.}\)

    5.

      Let \(X=[0,1]\cup(2,3)\) be given the subspace topology from \(\R\text{.}\) Consider the following subsets of \(X\text{.}\)
      \begin{equation*} Y=[0,1]\quad\text{and}\quad Z=(2,3) \end{equation*}
    • Both \(Y,Z\) are closed but not open.
    • The complement of \(Y\) (resp., \(Z\)) is open (resp., closed). Therefore, \(Y\) is closed (resp., open). Furthermore, as \(Y=(-1,1.5)\cap X\) (resp., \(Z=[1.5,3.5]\cap X\)), \(Y\) is open (resp., \(Z\) is closed.)
    • Both \(Y,Z\) are open as well as closed.
    • The complement of \(Y\) (resp., \(Z\)) is open (resp., closed). Therefore, \(Y\) is closed (resp., open). Furthermore, as \(Y=(-1,1.5)\cap X\) (resp., \(Z=[1.5,3.5]\cap X\)), \(Y\) is open (resp., \(Z\) is closed.)
    • The \(Y\) is closed but not open, and \(Z\) is open but not closed.
    • The complement of \(Y\) (resp., \(Z\)) is open (resp., closed). Therefore, \(Y\) is closed (resp., open). Furthermore, as \(Y=(-1,1.5)\cap X\) (resp., \(Z=[1.5,3.5]\cap X\)), \(Y\) is open (resp., \(Z\) is closed.)
    • The \(Z\) is closed but not open, and \(Y\) is open but not closed.
    • The complement of \(Y\) (resp., \(Z\)) is open (resp., closed). Therefore, \(Y\) is closed (resp., open). Furthermore, as \(Y=(-1,1.5)\cap X\) (resp., \(Z=[1.5,3.5]\cap X\)), \(Y\) is open (resp., \(Z\) is closed.)

    6.

      Consider \(X=\{(x,y)\in\R^2:x\geq 0\text{ and }y\geq 0\}\) with the subspace topology induced from \(\R^2\text{.}\) Then, \(Y=\{(x,y)\in X:0\leq x<2\text{ and }0\leq y<2\}\) is not an open subset of \(X\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • False.

    7.

      Let \((X,\tau)\) be a nonempty topological space with the indiscrete topology, i.e., \(\tau=\{\emptyset,X\}\text{.}\) For \(x\in X\text{,}\) the closure of \(\{x\}\) is \(X\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • If \(X=\{x\}\) then, we are done. So assume that there exists \(y\in X\) distinct from \(x\text{.}\) The only open set containing \(y\) is \(X\text{,}\) and thus, \(y\in\overline{\{x\}}\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • If \(X=\{x\}\) then, we are done. So assume that there exists \(y\in X\) distinct from \(x\text{.}\) The only open set containing \(y\) is \(X\text{,}\) and thus, \(y\in\overline{\{x\}}\text{.}\)

    8.

      The interior of rational (resp., irrational) numbers is nonempty.

    • True.

    • One may use density of rational numbers to conclude that the interior of \(\Q\) is empty.

    • False.

    • One may use density of rational numbers to conclude that the interior of \(\Q\) is empty.

    9.

      If \(A_n\) are subsets of \(\R\) then,
      \begin{equation*} \left(\bigcap A_n\right)^o=\bigcap A_n^o. \end{equation*}

    • True.

    • Consider sets \(A_n=(-1/n,1+1/n)\text{.}\) As \(A_n\) are open, \(A_n^o=A_n\) and hence, \(\cap A_n^o=\cap A_n=[0,1]\) which is a closed set. However, \(\left(\cap A_n\right)^o=\left([0,1]\right)^o=(0,1)\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Consider sets \(A_n=(-1/n,1+1/n)\text{.}\) As \(A_n\) are open, \(A_n^o=A_n\) and hence, \(\cap A_n^o=\cap A_n=[0,1]\) which is a closed set. However, \(\left(\cap A_n\right)^o=\left([0,1]\right)^o=(0,1)\text{.}\)

    10.

      The interior of \((0,1)\) is empty and its closure is \(\R\) when \(\R\) is given the cofinite topology.

    • True.

    • The interior of a set \(A\) is the largest open set contained in \(A\text{.}\) In the cofinite topology, every nonempty open set has a finite complement. Therefore, if a nonempty set \(U\) is an interior of \((0,1)\) then, \(\R\setminus U\) is a finite set. However, as \(\R\setminus (0,1)\subseteq\R\setminus U\text{,}\) and \(\R\setminus (0,1)\) is infinite, we must have \(U=\emptyset\text{.}\)
      Given any \(x\in\R\) and a nonempty open subset \(U\) containing \(x\text{,}\) \((0,1)\cap U\neq\emptyset\) because \(\R\setminus U\) is a finite set. Therefore, \(x\in\overline{(0,1)}\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • The interior of a set \(A\) is the largest open set contained in \(A\text{.}\) In the cofinite topology, every nonempty open set has a finite complement. Therefore, if a nonempty set \(U\) is an interior of \((0,1)\) then, \(\R\setminus U\) is a finite set. However, as \(\R\setminus (0,1)\subseteq\R\setminus U\text{,}\) and \(\R\setminus (0,1)\) is infinite, we must have \(U=\emptyset\text{.}\)
      Given any \(x\in\R\) and a nonempty open subset \(U\) containing \(x\text{,}\) \((0,1)\cap U\neq\emptyset\) because \(\R\setminus U\) is a finite set. Therefore, \(x\in\overline{(0,1)}\text{.}\)

    11.

      There exists a set \(A\) such that \(A,A^o,\overline{A},\overline{A^o}\) are all pairwise distinct.

    • True.

    • Consider \(A=[0,1)\cup(1,2]\cup\left(\Q\cap [3,4]\right)\cup\{5\}\) as a subset of \(\R\) with the usual topology. Here, \(A^o=(0,1)\cup (1,2)\text{,}\) \(\overline{A}=[0,2]\cup[3,4]\cup\{5\}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{A^o}=[0,2]\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Consider \(A=[0,1)\cup(1,2]\cup\left(\Q\cap [3,4]\right)\cup\{5\}\) as a subset of \(\R\) with the usual topology. Here, \(A^o=(0,1)\cup (1,2)\text{,}\) \(\overline{A}=[0,2]\cup[3,4]\cup\{5\}\text{,}\) and \(\overline{A^o}=[0,2]\text{.}\)

    12.

      Consider \(X=[0,1]\cup\{2\}\) as a metric space with the metric \(d(x,y)=|x-y|\text{.}\) Let \(S_2(0)=\{x\in X:d(x,0)=2\}\) (sphere in \(X\)) and let \(B_2(0)=\{x\in X:d(x,0)<2\}\) (ball in \(X\)). Then \(S_2(0)\cap \overline{B_2(0)}\neq\emptyset\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • Here \(B_2(0)=[0,1]\) and \(\overline{B_2(0)}=[0,1]\text{,}\) while \(S_2(0)=\{2\}\text{.}\)

    • False.

    • Here \(B_2(0)=[0,1]\) and \(\overline{B_2(0)}=[0,1]\text{,}\) while \(S_2(0)=\{2\}\text{.}\)

    13.

      Let \(X\subseteq\R\) be given the subspace topology. If \(X\) is a discrete subspace then, \(X\) is countable.

    • True.

    • For any \(x\in X\) there exists an open interval \(U_x\) such that \(U_x\cap X=\{x\}\) as the induced topology on \(X\) is discrete. For \(x\neq y\) we can choose smaller intervals, say \(x\in I_x\) and \(y\in I_y\) such that \(I_x\cap I_y=\emptyset\text{,}\) \(I_x\cap X=\{x\}\text{,}\) and \(I_y\cap X=\{y\}\text{.}\) For each \(x\in X\) pick \(q_x\in I_x\cap\Q\text{.}\) Then, the map \(X\to\Q\) given by \(x\mapsto q_x\) is injective.

    • False.

    • For any \(x\in X\) there exists an open interval \(U_x\) such that \(U_x\cap X=\{x\}\) as the induced topology on \(X\) is discrete. For \(x\neq y\) we can choose smaller intervals, say \(x\in I_x\) and \(y\in I_y\) such that \(I_x\cap I_y=\emptyset\text{,}\) \(I_x\cap X=\{x\}\text{,}\) and \(I_y\cap X=\{y\}\text{.}\) For each \(x\in X\) pick \(q_x\in I_x\cap\Q\text{.}\) Then, the map \(X\to\Q\) given by \(x\mapsto q_x\) is injective.

    14.

      Let \(X\) be a topological space. For any subset \(A\) of \(X\text{,}\) the boundary of \(A\) and the boundary of \(\overline{A}\) are the same.

    • True.

    • Let \(X\) be the real numbers with cofinite topology. Suppose that \(A=[0,1]\text{.}\) For any \(x\in\R\) and an open subset \(U_x\) containing \(x\text{.}\) As \(\R\setminus U_x\) is finite, \(U_x\cap [0,1]\neq\emptyset\text{.}\) Hence, \(\overline{A}=X\text{.}\) Similar reasoning show that there are no nonempty open subsets in \(A\text{,}\) i.e., \(A^o=\emptyset\text{.}\) Therefore, the boundary of \(A=\overline{A}\setminus A^o=X\text{,}\) while the boundary of \(\overline{A}=X=\emptyset\text{.}\)
      We remark that the above statement is true if \(X\) is assumed to be a Hausdorff space.

    • False.

    • Let \(X\) be the real numbers with cofinite topology. Suppose that \(A=[0,1]\text{.}\) For any \(x\in\R\) and an open subset \(U_x\) containing \(x\text{.}\) As \(\R\setminus U_x\) is finite, \(U_x\cap [0,1]\neq\emptyset\text{.}\) Hence, \(\overline{A}=X\text{.}\) Similar reasoning show that there are no nonempty open subsets in \(A\text{,}\) i.e., \(A^o=\emptyset\text{.}\) Therefore, the boundary of \(A=\overline{A}\setminus A^o=X\text{,}\) while the boundary of \(\overline{A}=X=\emptyset\text{.}\)
      We remark that the above statement is true if \(X\) is assumed to be a Hausdorff space.

    15.

      Let \((X,d)\) be a metric space. Suppose that \(B_r(x), B_s(y)\) be open balls of radii \(r,s\text{,}\) respectively. If \(B_r(x)\subsetneq B_s(y)\) then, \(r<2s\text{.}\)

    • True.

    • Let \(z\in B_s(y)\setminus B_r(x)\text{.}\) Thus, \(r\leq d(x,z)\text{.}\) We thus have the following inequalities.
      \begin{equation*} r\leq d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)< 2s \end{equation*}

    • False.

    • Let \(z\in B_s(y)\setminus B_r(x)\text{.}\) Thus, \(r\leq d(x,z)\text{.}\) We thus have the following inequalities.
      \begin{equation*} r\leq d(x,z)\leq d(x,y)+d(y,z)< 2s \end{equation*}
